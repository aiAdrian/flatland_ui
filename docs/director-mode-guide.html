Director Mode, End‑to‑End

In Director mode the human stops making per-train decisions and instead issues a directive: three weighted goals. The AI plans complete schedules against those goals, drives the trains autonomously, re-plans when the world disagrees, and exposes its plan, reasoning and alternatives for supervision. This guide walks the whole loop through the actual components and endpoints in the repo — and then opens the planner up, down to real, cross-checked searches on a generated network. It assumes you know Flatland and the dispatcher UI; everything else is explained here.

01

Entering Director mode: the control handover

The three interaction modes live in one union (InteractionMode in interaction-modes.ts): recommendation (AI suggests, human decides), co-learning (neutral options, human reflects) and director — “the AI runs autonomously on your directives; you supervise.” Switching to Director is a real transfer of control, handled by SessionStore.setInteractionMode():

  • The current policy is remembered in _policyBeforeDirector, then the session policy is switched to goal_directed via POST /session/{id}/policy (backend-first — the UI only reflects a confirmed switch).
  • Entering does not auto-play: the human first reviews the directive, then explicitly starts the run from the directive card. Leaving Director while playing pauses, so the human regains step-by-step control, and the remembered policy is restored — unless the user deliberately picked another policy while inside Director, in which case that choice wins.
  • A session created while Director mode is active is born under goal_directed, so it runs the Director’s planner from its very first step.
  • aiInControl is computed from the mode and gates the rest of the HMI: option presentation becomes 'none' (no per-decision prompting), and the “why?” rationale prompt for manual overrides is suppressed.
02

The directive: three dials and a start button

The directive has two frontend surfaces:

Director Weights panel human input

director-weights.component.ts exposes the three planner dials — punctuality, connections, stability — as 0–5 point dot meters. Only the ratio matters (the backend normalises), and bucketing into points makes every click a change the planner can actually respond to. Mechanics:

  • A dial change is debounced 400 ms, then pushed as POST /director/weights with plan: true — the response carries the fresh plan and its drawable paths, so scorecard and map overlay update the moment the slider settles.
  • The panel polls GET /session/{id}/director every 2 s to stay fresh (autonomous re-plans happen without the panel’s involvement). A poll never overwrites an in-flight push, and backend dials are only adopted when the user isn’t mid-drag.
  • The Director plans lazily, so the panel nudges a first plan once per session — a plan-less session (no models, unroutable) is not retry-looped.
  • Hovering the panel sets directorPlanHover, which lights up the committed plan’s train paths on the map — the gut check for what the dials produced.
  • The panel also hosts the supervision buttons: Verify, Re-plan now and What-if (§14), plus the plan scorecard: provenance badge (search / baseline / fallback), weighted score, per-axis utilities, the decision trace and the re-plan history.

Directive card human input

director-directive.component.ts summarises the directive — KPI priorities and the policy the AI runs — and holds the explicit Start / Resume button, which calls store.play(activePolicy, playSpeed). This replaces per-decision prompting.

03

Planning: model-guided search over a decision-point graph

GoalDirectedPolicy.reset(env) plans once per env (results are cached weak-keyed per env — session APIs rebuild the policy object on every request, so nothing lives on the instance). The pipeline, in backend/app/policies/goal_based_policies/:

  1. Compress the grid — infrastructure_graph.build_decision_point_graph reduces the rail grid to the cells where a train can stand and decide: stations and the wait cells in front of switches.
  2. Walk the decisions — the greedy strategy (search.py, runtime default; beam and MCTS exist) processes decisions chronologically via branching.next_decision. Every node a train reaches (stations and switch wait cells — the places a train can stand) is a decision: the hold menu (WAIT_MENU, 0/1/3/5/10 extra steps) crossed with up to 3 route alternatives where the track offers a choice, hold-only where it does not. The whole walk is capped at 64 decisions.
  3. Complete every option — complete_from extends each option into a full candidate plan with naive chained shortest paths (a deliberate, documented decision against integrating Tokener’s PP/CBS for this leaf-estimate role).
  4. Score all candidates — the weighted ensemble (§10). Overlap-free options are preferred (has_planned_overlap); when every option collides on paper, the score decides alone rather than pruning the search into a corner.
  5. Commit the best branch and continue to the next decision.
  6. Portfolio guarantee — director_plan holds the searched plan against the two baseline planners (plan_all_lines, plan_avoiding_overlaps) under the same weighted score. By its own judgment the Director never returns a plan worse than what already existed; ties prefer the search, whose trace explains itself.

The winner becomes a DirectorPlan — schedules, source (search / lines / avoidance), score, the per-decision trace (every option with its three utilities and weighted score, which were considered, which was chosen — the HMI’s “why this route” material) — and is handed to a SchedulePlayer that will drive it step by step.

04

Decision points: one switch, all six tiles

Ground rule for everything below: a decision exists only at a decision point — a station, or a directional wait cell (blue squares) — and every decision point a train reaches is a decision: hold-only where a single route continues, hold × route where the track forks ahead. Switch tiles (red squares) are never decision points; nothing is ever decided on them. An edge is the full driven path from one decision point to the next, through however many switches lie on the way — picking an edge implicitly sets every turnout along it.

08

The graph being built, on a real env

No schematic — this is build_decision_point_graph running on a real generated env, drawn by the repo’s own visualization.py, one build step per panel: ① find the switch cells; ② for every trailing (merging) approach of a switch, mark the neighbouring tile as a wait cell for that one heading (find_switch_decision_cells — the arrows); ③ add the stations; ④ walk the rails from every node, in its direction of travel, to the next relevant node — one directed edge per distinct path. Every orange circle and green square in the final panel is a decision point: hold-only when a single edge leaves it, hold × route when several do.

Three rules to read the planner by

  • Decision points are the whole story. Stations and directional wait cells — nothing else exists for the search. Switch tiles never appear in the action space: nothing is ever decided on a junction.
  • An edge is a whole path, not a hop. It runs to the next decision point through any number of switches, and choosing it sets every turnout on the way — which is why ladders of facing switches (§06) collapse into one fan-out at the decision point before them, and why hold × edge at a single decision point is coherent: the hold happens where the train stands, the edge resolves everything until the next place it can stand.
  • Choice-less decision points decide their hold. A wait cell with one way onward gets a hold-only decision (the wait menu against its single edge), and the origin decides too — a departure hold is a legitimate move. Wait cells never sit on a switch: where the tile before a junction is itself a switch, the wait cell sits on the first plain-rail tile of each feeding branch. Measured on 4 generated networks: 14–18 decisions per plan (~21% hold-only) against the 64-decision budget.
09

One real greedy run, move by move

This is the runtime default — greedy_search — doing what the Director actually does when it plans, shown decision by decision on the same env. The loop, straight from search.py: next_decision picks the earliest open decision across all trains → _expand_and_score completes every option into a full two-train plan and scores it with the ensemble → overlap-free candidates outrank colliding ones, the highest weighted score among them is committed (ties: shortest hold, cheapest route) → the winner becomes the new state, and the next decision may belong to the other train. 10

The score: two learned models, one computed measure

Every candidate plan gets three utilities in [0, 1] (ensemble.score_candidates), combined as a weighted sum under the normalised dials. Punctuality and connections are predicted by trained networks; stability is computed — a deterministic static analysis with no model and no rollout.

Punctuality

ScheduleEvaluator learned
U = 0.5·P(all arrive) + 0.5·Σ p_b·u_b

Graph-encoder + per-train GRU. Two heads: probability all trains arrive, and a distribution over six delay buckets (0–4 … 60+ min), folded with bucket utilities.

Connections

ConnectionTransformer learned
U = geomean( P(transfer i survives) )

Each planned transfer is its own token attending to its two trains, its station and competing transfers; a per-token head yields a calibrated survival probability.

Stability

assess_safety computed
U = slack · deadlock · track · cascade

Static analysis of the plan’s own timeline — how much disturbance it can absorb. No rollout: a plan that survives only via the reactive safety net should score unsafe.

weighted = w_p·U_punct + w_c·U_conn + w_s·U_stab   (w normalised from the dials)

Inside the stability measure

Four survival probabilities from safety.py, multiplied — so one catastrophic element (a zero-slack train, a two-minute opposing meet) tanks the score. The veto semantics live here, which is why the top-level combination can stay a plain weighted sum.

SlackPer-train buffer to the latest arrival; fragility decays exp(−slack/τ). One zero-slack train caps the product.
DeadlockOpposing trains on a shared stretch: clearing gap → exp(−gap/τ), halved when a reroute exists.
TrackPeak trains a 15-minute blockage window catches per shared stretch (sweep-line over presence intervals).
CascadeFollow-gap couplings; a reference disturbance propagates, shrinking per hop — score falls with mean reach.

safety = slack × deadlock × track × cascade

Parameters live in one SafetyParams dataclass, and safety_calibration.py validates the measure empirically: rank correlation of 1 − safety with damage under malfunction-disturbed rollouts, against confounds.

Where the number comes from, precisely

Every score in the storyboard tables (§09) is one call to ensemble.score_candidates: three utilities in [0, 1], each with its own machinery, combined by the Director’s weights.

  • Punctuality learned — the ScheduleEvaluator’s two heads on the candidate: U = 0.5·P(all arrive) + 0.5·Σ p_b·u_b over six delay buckets with linear utilities (1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0.0). The 50/50 blend (ARRIVAL_BLEND) keeps both failure modes visible: arriving very late, and being punctual while losing trains.
  • Connections learned — the ConnectionTransformer predicts a survival probability per planned transfer; the utility is their geometric mean (clamped at 10⁻⁴ so one hopeless transfer scores near zero instead of erasing all others). Geometric on purpose: an arithmetic mean saturates near 1 and stops steering exactly when the dial is turned up, while one doomed transfer moves the geometric mean the way a broken connection is actually experienced. The plain mean survives in the breakdown as kept_ratio — the number to report, not to optimise. No planned transfers → 1.0 by definition, which is why the violet bars in the figure are pinned at 1.0: this demo env plans none.
  • Stability computed — no model: assess_safety’s product of four survival factors (slack · deadlock · track · cascade), so one catastrophic element tanks the whole axis.

The combination is deliberately a plain weighted sum — weighted = w_p·U_p + w_c·U_c + w_s·U_s — so the Director dials behave predictably; the veto semantics already live inside the stability product. The weights are the mode’s core lever: the HMI dials (0–5 points each) go over the wire as-is, DirectorWeights accepts any non-negative scale and normalises to sum 1 at scoring time — only the ratio matters. This whole storyboard ran at the default 1 : 1 : 1, so each axis counts one third; drag the stability dial to 5 with the rest at 1 and the same candidates get re-ranked with w = (1/7, 1/7, 5/7) — that is what a mid-episode weight push re-plans with.

11

The MCTS strategy: exploring before committing

The mcts strategy (search.py; greedy is the runtime default, this is the third strategy, simulations=32, c_puct=1.4) plans over exactly the decision points built in §04–§08 — same next_decision, same hold × route options. A tree node is a search state: the committed prefixes of both trains. Its children are the options of the earliest open decision across the two trains, so a path down the tree interleaves T0 and T1 chronologically — one tree plans the fleet, not one per train. Per root decision it runs 32 simulations (select by PUCT → expand one state, scoring all its options as completed plans → leaf value = best option’s ensemble score → backpropagate), commits the most-visited root option, and advances. 12

Driving: the autonomous play loop

The Start button (or the toolbar’s play) posts /session/{id}/play with policy: goal_directed. The endpoint gates on the session’s control-policy set (enabled_policy_ids, same gate as /step and /policy) — goal_directed is control-enabled but deliberately not scenario-capable.

The endpoint spawns _play_loop (websockets.py) as an asyncio task: build the runtime policy, wrap it in OverridePolicy (so human per-train overrides still win if used), then step at the configured speed until done or paused, broadcasting each state over the session WebSocket. Every action request inside the loop goes through GoalDirectedPolicy.act_for_handle, which does two things: run the once-per-step re-planning hook _maybe_replan(env) (§13), then ask the SchedulePlayer for the train’s next action on its committed schedule. A train the planner could not route holds (DO_NOTHING) rather than guessing.

13

Staying on course: mid-episode re-planning

The committed plan is not sacred. Three triggers start a residual re-plan (replan.py), all funnelled through the same machinery:

  • Malfunction gated — a fresh malfunction of consequence, detected once per step, respecting a 10-step cooldown after any re-plan.
  • Weight change ungated — a dial moved mid-episode marks the weights dirty; the user changed the objective, so no cooldown and no gate: trading delay away may be exactly what the new dials ask for.
  • Manual “Re-plan now” gated — the panel button, synchronous via POST /director/replan.

The residual problem is deliberately expressed in the language the value models already speak: a full-horizon schedule per train in which everything up to the current position is pinned, and reality’s delay becomes a virtual wait at the train’s frontier node. The installed checkpoints stay valid — no retraining, no new input scheme. capture_progress reads the live player; residual_plan runs the ordinary search from those seeds and holds the result against the continue candidate (the do-nothing baseline). Mid-episode, ties keep the current plan: churn needs to pay for itself.

Background execution and re-anchoring

Planning costs seconds, so autonomous triggers run the search on a background thread while the current plan keeps driving (at most one job in flight). By the time a result lands, the trains have moved — so _finish_replan re-anchors it to a fresh capture: a train whose pinned prefix is still a prefix of the new route joins the new plan; a train that already drove past a divergence point keeps its current plan. (Applying stale tails blindly was the frozen-trains bug.)

The rollout gate

The models rank residual plans optimistically — they over-choose “research”. So gated re-plans are committed on simulated outcomes, not scores: rollout_gate forward-runs both branches on forks and only lets the switch through if it actually beats continuing. Every event — committed, kept, or vetoed — is recorded in the plan info’s replans history with reason, source, scores and gate verdict; the weights panel renders it as the re-plan timeline.

14

Supervision: seeing what the Director sees

Adjustable autonomy needs inspectable autonomy. Four surfaces let the human audit the plan without touching the live episode:

Map overlay hover

Hovering the weights panel draws the committed plan’s per-train paths on the map (flatland-map reads directorPlanPaths, gated by directorPlanHover). Paths come from the live player’s own progress (SchedulePlayer.future_path): only what is still ahead, starting at each train’s actual position — contiguous on the rails however delayed the run is.

Verify ground truth

POST /director/verify replays the committed schedules on a pristine fork (persister clone, state-only reset, step zero) and reports what actually happens — total delay, arrivals, bucket, connections kept, measured safety — side by side with what the models predicted. Prediction vs reality, on demand.

What-if forked futures

POST /director/whatif forks the live episode twice: branch A continues the committed plan, branch B re-plans under candidate weights; both are simulated to the episode’s end with the same future malfunction stream and reported side by side. Nothing is committed. This is the A3S restore → simulate-forward → report contract (consortium reference agent-as-a-service-trace-rl) implemented in-process — the seam is kept narrow so the Redis-backed service could replace it.

Consistent forecasts everywhere

In Director mode the trains follow the committed plan — so every other forecasting surface must too. director_replay_factory hands out a cheap, model-free DirectorPlanReplayPolicy that snapshots the live player and replays the plan on forked envs: scenario forecasts use it as the baseline (hmi.py), and the Co-Learning override what-if consults it as well. Re-plans and weight pushes invalidate cached scenario forecasts so no stale future survives a plan change.

16

The degradation ladder

Every failure mode steps down to something safe and honest — the plan info always says what is actually driving:

No model checkpoints installed The planner falls back to plan_avoiding_overlaps, recorded as “avoidance (no models)”. Degraded, never broken.
Search fails at plan time Same fallback path; the portfolio baselines exist independently of the models.
A train cannot be routed It holds (DO_NOTHING) rather than guessing; source recorded as “unroutable” when nothing is plannable.
A residual re-plan fails or is vetoed The committed plan keeps driving; the event lands in the history so the human sees the attempt.
Trains diverged while the background search ran Re-anchoring keeps every diverged train on its current plan; if nobody can join, the re-plan quietly becomes a “continue”.

That is the whole loop: the human states what matters; the Director searches, scores, drives, adapts — and keeps every decision, score and alternative on the table for the human who remains in charge of the objective.
